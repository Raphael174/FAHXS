• General correlations are developed and verified for friction factor and 
heat-transfer coefficient for single-phase turbulent flow in internally aug- 
mented tubes. Data from frequently referred investigations were gathered 
for a wide range of tube parameters with e/d, 0.01 to 0.2; p/d, 0.1 to 7.0; 
a/90, 0.3 to 1.0, and flow parameters Re, 5000 to 250,000 and Pr, 0.66 to 
37.6. The data were applied to a linear model to obtain normalized 
correlations that were then modified to approach smooth tube correlation 
as the roughness variables became very small. A shape function was 
included in the friction correlation to account for different rib profiles. 
These correlations were then verified by independent experiments. Differ- 
ent types of commercial fibbed tubes were tested with water as working 
fluid under heating conditions. Five wire coil inserts were tested in another 
apparatus using air under cooling conditions. The parameters tested were 
Re = 5000-60,000, e/d = 0.023-0.127, p/d = 0.17-1.12, and a/90 = 
0.34-0.91. The results proved that the correlations can be successfully 
applied to a wide range of roughness types and Prandtl number. The 
present correlations are superior, for this large database, to those pre- 
sented by other investigators. The friction factor correlation predicts 96% 
of the database to within + 50% and 77% of the database to within + 20%. 
Corresponding prediction figures for the heat-transfer correlation are 99 
and 69%.

INTRODUCTION 
Thermal system performance and efficiency can be vastly 
improved, thereby reducing the size of heat exchangers. 
The augmentation (or enhancement) of heat transfer has 
become an important factor in achieving these goals [1]. 
The performance of heat exchangers, for single-phase 
flows in particular, can be improved by many active and 
passive augmentation techniques. The former uses surface 
modification or an additional device incorporated into the 
equipment. The existing boundary layer is disturbed and 
the heat-transfer performance is improved, usually with 
an increase in the flow friction and pressure drop. Typical 
examples of passive augmentation are surface roughness, 
displaced promoters, 
and vortex generators. 
Surface 
roughness--introduced through knurling or threading or 
formed by repeated ribs--promotes augmentation through 
the disturbance of the sublayer that is close to the surface. 
These methods are used to improve heat-transfer coeffi- 
cients inside tubes [2] or outside tubes or rods [3]. Dis- 
placed promoters include inserts that alter flow mechanics 
near the surface by disturbing the core flow. Examples are 
baffles [4] and mixing elements [5]. Vortex flow can be 
created through coiled wires [6], stationary propellers [7], 
or twisted tapes [8]. Active augmentation, which has also 
been studied extensively, requires the addition of external

Of the several methods discussed above, the most popu-

effectiveness in enhancing the heat transfer and its sim-

and brazed together to obtain any desired length. But this 
method of fabrication is suitable only for laboratory test- 
ing. Production on a large scale may be carried out

smooth profile. High production rates usually dictate heli- 
cal ribs; a feed is given to the tube to form the desired 
helix angle. Here the roughness height, pitch, helix angle, 
and to a large extent, the profile shapes are controlled by

alternatively, the heat duty of a fixed area heat exchanger

geothermal, and ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC) 
power plants, which require very large heat exchangers. 
BACKGROUND 
In spite of the increasing use of these augmented tubes, 
there are no truly general correlations available for quan- 
titative assessment of pressure drop and heat transfer. In 
one of the early studies on ribbed tubes, Kalinin et al. [16] 
proposed correlations for pressure drop and heat transfer. 
However, the correlations are valid only for transverse 
ribs within specific ranges of e/d and p/d. 
Many of the studies found in the literature have tried to 
extend the studies of Nikuradse [17] on friction with

of roughness. Webb et al. [19] presented correlations for

The most general correlations to date were proposed by 
Withers [21, 22], Li et al. [23], and Nakayama et al. [24] for 
helically ribbed tubes. Withers obtained data for single- 
start and multiple-start ribs produced by indenting. The 
friction factor was correlated for each tube individually, 
resulting in distinct functions of tube parameters. The 
heat-transfer-momentum-transfer analogy was then used 
to obtain separate heat-transfer correlations for single- 
start and multiple-start ridges. The major disadvantage of 
this correlation is that it is accurate only for tubes for 
which the operands are known beforehand, although an 
estimate can be made for them by correlating the 
operands, r and m, with tube variables. The correlation 
developed by Li et al. [23], which was also based on 
sand-grain roughness and analogy considerations, includes 
the effects of pitch and angle. Their data for helix angles 
less 60 ° were very limited. The correlations of Nakayama 
et al. [24] are presented for several ranges of helix angle 
based on postulated flow patterns. Although the existence 
of different flow patterns has been acknowledged widely, 
flow categorization depends on many factors, such as Re, 
p/e, shape of the profile, and, of course, the helix angle. 
Further, the angle of fluid rotation may be different from 
the helix angle. Also, the correlation is restricted to a 
narrow range of 11 < p/e < 14, thereby reducing its ef- 
fectiveness. 
Careful scrutiny of the correlations mentioned above 
reveals that the different effects of pitch and helix angle 
are included cumulatively to the basic e/d correlation. 
This may not be justified since the inclusion of pitch and 
helix angle introduces rotation into the flow, thereby 
effectively changing the crossover flow mechanism on 
which the analogy method was based. Moreover, the anal- 
ogy considerations are based on the assumptions of log-law 
distributions in velocity and temperature that exist beyond 
the rib height and also on the turbulent Pr being equal to 
unity. Studies by Bremhorst and Bullock [25], while con- 
firming the presence of some form of thermal and hydro- 
dynamic similarities in flows downstream of a roughness 
element, note the absence of any suitable scaling laws to 
collapse the developing profiles into a single function. 
They also report that the turbulent Pr varies widely from 
0.4 to 1.2. These findings deviate from the assumptions 
employed in developing the analogy; thus, further studies 
may be needed before this method can be applied to more 
complicated flows such as those found in helical repeated 
rib tubes. 
Perhaps the weakest point in these previous investiga- 
tions, however, is the lack of any testing of the correla- 
tions to data outside of the particular study. This appears 
to be an opportune time to develop a general correlation 
that describes the vast data now available for this type of 
tube. 
The objective of the present study is to develop more 
accurate correlations for friction factor and heat-transfer 
coefficient for internally enhanced tube single-phase flows. 
Because of the wide range of variables involved and the 
unique nature of the flow mechanisms involved, a statisti- 
cal approach has been adopted in this investigation. 
DATA BASE DEVELOPMENT 
The first phase of this study involved the development of a 
database from a pool of well-established investigations for 
single-phase turbulent flow of Newtonian fluids. Care was 
taken to cover a wide range of tube and flow parameters 
in order to obtain an unbiased database, with about 98% 
of the data falling in the following ranges: 
e/d, 0.01 to 0.2; p/d, 0.1 to 7.0; 
a/90, 0.3 to 1.0; 
Re, 5000 to 250,000; 
Pr, 0.66 to 37.6. 
The various investigations (26-35) involved in the devel- 
opment of the friction and heat transfer data sets are 
described in Table 1. 
The database for frictional pressure drop contains more 
than 1650 points. The data set consists of various tube 
parameters such as roughness height and roughness pitch, 
both normalized with envelope diameter as recommended 
by Marner et al. [36], helix angle, and profile shape, along 
with the Reynolds number and the pressure drop informa- 
tion. The frictional pressure drop itself is presented in the 
form of Fanning friction factor, also defined in terms of 
envelope diameter. Although most of the pressure drop 
experiments were conducted under isothermal conditions, 
nonisothermal data are also included in the data set, but 
only for e/d > 0.01 end p/d > 0.1, since the temperature 
effects on friction with large roughness are extremely 
small. The breakdown of the data set according to the 
profile shape is as follows: semi-circular, 1060; wire coils, 
331; rectangular, 188; and triangular, 79. The increase in 
friction factor ratio for the augmented tubes above the 
smooth tube value ranges from 1.0 to 15. 
As an example of the variations involved in the data, 
Figs. 2 and 3 show the dependence of f on Re for tubes 
having nearly the same geometry from three studies. The 
spread of the data suggests that it will be difficult to get 
more accurate correlations. Even though some data sets 
might be suspect because of such factors as inaccurate 
measurement equipment or a high heat balance error, no 
specific attempt could be made to judge the quality of the 
data set and to quantify any inherent errors. 
The heat transfer database has more than 1800 points. 
It was designed similarly to the friction data set but with 
additional information on the Prandtl number. The heat- 
transfer coefficient is presented in the form of the Stanton 
number, which is again defined in terms of envelope 
diameter. Most of the data are from experiments con- 
ducted under uniform heat flux boundary conditions, with 
the exception of one [21, 22] although data from one 
constant temperature study are included. The number of 
points in each shape category is as follows: semi-circular, 
1175; wire coils, 327; rectangular, 225; and triangular, 80. 
The heat-transfer increase varies from one to five times 
the smooth tube heat-transfer coefficient.

EXPERIMENTAL VERIFICATION 
Water Apparatus 
The experiments in this rig were conducted to test the 
correlations against data for several commercially avail- 
able enhanced tubes. These tubes varied widely in dimen- 
sions and patterns. The test setup was designed to provide 
controlled heating of the water by using direct current 
heating of the tube wall. A schematic diagram of the 
experimental setup is shown in Fig. 4. Water from the 
positive displacement pump flowed through calibrated flow 
meters and into the test section. Rubber hoses electrically 
and thermally isolated the test section from the rest of the 
loop. The water exiting the test section was cooled by a 
counterflow heat exchanger before being returned to the 
pump. A deionizing unit was installed in a bypass loop to 
deionize the water, and an accumulator reduced the pres- 
sure fluctuations created by the pump. A surge tank, 
located at a higher elevation and open to the atmosphere, 
was attached to the suction side of the pump. A 3-kW 
heating coil was incorporated into this tank for degassing 
purposes. 
Three copper-constantin (30 gage) thermocouples, in- 
stalled circumferentially at each of five stations, measured 
the tube wall temperatures. The thermocouple reading 
and the power through the test section were measured by

ing a seamless tube, e.g., [21, 22], Tubes 3 and 4 were 
welded from formed plates. During the first phase of 
fabrication, a thin sheet of metal was fed through ribbed 
rollers, forming the desired ribs or flutes on the plates. In 
the second phase, these ribbed plates were rolled at an 
angle over a mandrel and welded at the seam. This 
method can produce ribs of extreme dimensions, for ex- 
ample, p/e < 5. 
Experimentation consisted of several steps, including 
the removal of air, degassing of water, heat-transfer exper- 
iments, and isothermal pressure-drop studies. After the 
test section was connected to the rest of the loop, the 
surge tank was filled with distilled water. A small sight 
glass made of Plexiglas that was attached to the exit of the 
tube was used to detect any bubble in the flow. The lines 
were cleared of air bubbles before they were connected to 
the pressure taps. In the second stage, water was deminer- 
alized as it passed through the deionizer. After 30 min, 
the degassing heater was switched on and the water was 
heated to its boiling point. Care was taken to circulate 
water through all the flow meters, the filter, the test 
section, and other units. The water in the surge tank was 
heated, driving out the dissolved gases, until no further 
bubbles were noted in either the sight glass or the flow 
meters.

PRACTICAL SIGNIFICANCE/USEFULNESS 
The study presents a state-of-the-art database for friction 
and heat transfer for turbulent flows in enhanced tubes. 
The heat exchanger designers and researchers can use the 
database to compare the performance of their tubes. The 
general correlations are applicable for a wide range of 
parameters. Unlike the existing correlations, the present 
correlations decouple the prediction of the friction factor 
and heat-transfer coefficient The advantage is more accu- 
rate estimates as any error introduced into the friction 
factor prediction is not propagated into the heat-transfer 
coefficient estimates. The general nature of the correla- 
tions is convenient for designers in arriving at optimum 
roughness parameters. Furthermore, the asymptotic na- 
ture enables the application of the correlations to very 
small roughness heights. The presence of a shape function 
helps compare the relative performance of enhanced tube 
with different roughness profiles. It is anticipated that the 
correlations, the databases, and the new data from the 
comprehensive verification experiments present the de- 
signers with information necessary for improved design 
and performance of their products.

CONCLUSIONS 
1. General correlations for friction factor and heat trans- 
fer that are applicable to a very wide range of inter- 
nally ribbed tubes are presented. These correlations 
should be useful in the design of heat exchangers for a 
wide variety of energy systems. 
2. The inclusion of a shape function resulted in a better 
correlation of the friction factor data but not of the 
heat-transfer data. Moreover, the reduction of the cor- 
relations to the smooth tube condition with decreasing 
roughness severity adds to the utility of the correla- 
tions. 
3. Both the pressure-drop and the heat-transfer correla- 
tions apply well for the coil inserts. 
4. Based on the limited study, heating or cooling appears 
to have only marginal impact on the pressure drop for 
the roughness range considered. 
5. For ribs with varying pitch and angle, the systems are 
nonsimilar, and the analogy method does not result in 
particularly accurate correlations, as shown by the 
quantitative assessment. 
6. The experiments confirm the relative accuracy of the 
correlations when applied to a wide range of roughness 
types and roughness parameters as found in typical, 
commercially available, enhanced tubes. Predictions of 
the heat-transfer correlation are better than the fric- 
tion factor predictions for both air and water experi- 
ments. The relative accuracy of the present correlations 
offers strong arguments for a statistical approach in 
forming generalized correlations for channel geome- 
tries.
